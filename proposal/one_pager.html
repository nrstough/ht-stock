A 90-day pilot to cut prepared-food waste

I have built a tool that tells the kitchen how much to make each morning, based on what actually sold on days like tomorrow. I am asking to test it at this store for 90 days. If it works, we throw away less food without leaving the case empty.

[Your name, role] · September 2026

What I need from you

  1. Permission, in writing, to use this store's daily sales for the pilot items. Read-only, and only for the pilot.
  2. A daily count of what we put out for those items. A photo of a clipboard is enough. Without this we cannot measure waste, so nothing else works.
  3. About 40 to 80 hours of off-floor time spread over the 90 days.
  4. Agreement now on what counts as success, and on recognition if we get there.

The problem

The par sheet sets a fixed quantity for each item. It cannot see that tomorrow is rainy, or a payday, or the day before a holiday. So we make too much on slow days and sell out on busy ones. Industry figures put prepared-food waste at 15 to 25 percent of everything we make.

What already exists

  • A phone app that logs what gets thrown out. Two weeks of use gives this store's real waste figure in dollars per year.
  • A forecasting model that turns sales history, the calendar and the weather into a morning make-list. It was built and tested on a simulated store, so no company data has been used.
  • The connection to a real sales export. It is written and tested against a mock file. It has never run on real data, because I have not asked yet. This document is the ask.

What the test showed

Over one full simulated year, compared with a well-run par sheet:

−37%waste, with the shelves kept as full as they are today
−60%waste, if we accept slightly more sellouts
13%forecast error, against 15.5% for a simple trailing average

Simulated store, simulated dollars. They show the method works. This store's real number comes from the pilot, and I am promising 10 to 15 percent, not 37.

How the pilot runs

Weeks 1–2

Measure. Staff log waste with the app. Result: our real baseline, in dollars per year.

Weeks 3–6

Watch. The model prints a make-list every morning. The kitchen ignores it. We score its predictions against real sales.

Weeks 7–13

Follow. The kitchen uses the list. The manager can override any line. We measure waste against the baseline.

The off-ramp. Before the kitchen changes anything, the model has to pass five checks we agree on now. If it fails any of them, we stop at week 6 and nothing on the floor has changed.

Success means waste dollars down at least 10 percent against the baseline, with no real drop in what is on the shelf.

What the kitchen sees

One printed sheet a day. No new screens and no new system.

CHECK WHAT IS LEFT FIRST. Bread loaf 30 10 3-day shelf life: one day's demand

What could go wrong

  • The forecast may be worse on real data than in the simulation. That is what weeks 3 to 6 are for. Nothing changes in the kitchen until it has proved itself.
  • The shelves could run short. The pilot uses the setting that keeps availability where it is today, and any item can go back to its par the same day.
  • The first real sales file will probably need cleanup. The import step refuses bad data and says what is wrong. I have budgeted the first week of the watch phase for this.
Two things it does not handle yet. Items that keep for several days, like bread and cake, get a reminder on the sheet rather than a quantity. And weekly ad items will forecast worse than the rest. Both are stated in the plan rather than hidden.

The payoff

At 10 to 15 percent of a baseline of [$ baseline] per year, the pilot pays for its hours in the first weeks of live use. The same model works for every item and every store, so a district of [≈20] stores is where the real money is. The pilot turns that from an estimate into a measured number.
