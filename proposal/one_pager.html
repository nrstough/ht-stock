Fresh Forecast: a 90-day pilot to cut prepared-food waste

Every night we throw away food we made too much of. Every week we send customers home from an empty case. Both are the same problem: the par sheet can't see tomorrow. A forecast can. The software is built. I need permission to point it at one store.

What the proof of concept showed

A simulated store ran for three years. The model learned from the first two and was judged on the third, against a well-run par sheet. Waste (at retail value) over that year:

$138KPar sheet, today's method
18.8% of production wasted
−37%Model, with shelf availability held at today's level
−60%Model at the profit-optimal setting (availability 98% → 96%)

These are simulated dollars. They prove the method works, not what this store will save. That is why the pilot promises 10–15%, not 37–60%, and why it is judged against the store's own measured baseline.

What the kitchen would actually see

One printed sheet each morning. No new screens, no new system. The manager can override any line, and overrides are recorded.

The 90-day plan

Weeks 1–2
Baseline. Log every discarded item on 5–10 pilot products with the waste tool. Output: this store's real waste number in dollars per year.
Weeks 3–6
Shadow mode. The model trains on this store's sales history and prints its sheet every morning. The kitchen ignores it. Each forecast is scored against what really sold.
Weeks 7–13
Live. The kitchen follows the sheet. Waste and sellouts are measured against the baseline.
The go/no-go is fixed in advance. Five pass/fail checks print on every weekly shadow report from week one: data is complete, forecasts beat the par sheet, forecast ranges are honest, projected waste saving is at least 15% with no rise in sellouts, and the big-dollar items are covered. If any check fails, we stop at week 6 and nothing in the kitchen has changed.

Success in the live phase: at least a 10% cut in prepared-food waste dollars versus the baseline, with no material drop in on-shelf availability.

What could go wrong

  • Forecasts don't transfer from simulation to this store. Shadow mode is the gate. Four weeks of scored predictions before production changes.
  • Customers find empty cases. The pilot runs at the availability-held setting. Sellouts are tracked daily and any item can go back to its par instantly.
  • The kitchen doesn't trust the sheet. Overrides are a feature, logged rather than fought. The pilot measures how it would really run.
  • The first real export breaks the importer. Likely. The import step refuses bad data and names the problem rather than training on it. Budget the first week of shadow mode for this.
Two honest limits. Items that keep for several days (bread, cake) are printed as a check-what's-left reminder, not a make quantity; the model doesn't yet forecast them usefully. And weekly ad items aren't modelled, so on a heavy ad week the forecast for those items will be noisier.

If it works

At a conservative 10–15% against a measured baseline of [$ baseline] per year, the pilot pays back its hours in the first weeks of live operation. The same model serves every item, so it scales from one store to a district of [≈20] stores without redesign. At district scale, simulation-grade performance would be worth six figures a year. The pilot is how we replace "would be" with a measured fact.

Method, in one line: a demand model reads each item's recent sales, the calendar, and the weather forecast, predicts tomorrow's demand as a range, and a cost rule picks the quantity where one more unit wasted costs the same as one more sale missed.
