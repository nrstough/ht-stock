Proposal · Prepared foods · Store [#]

A 90-day pilot to reduce prepared-food waste

I have built a tool that works out how much of each item the kitchen should make, based on what sold on similar days in the past. I would like to test it at this store over 90 days, with a fixed stopping point if it does not perform. This page sets out what I am asking for, why I think each request is reasonable, and what the store gets in return.

Nathan Stough, Harris Teeter associate · September 2026

The problem

A par sheet fixes a quantity for each item and leaves it there. It cannot account for weather, paydays or the run-up to a holiday, so we make too much on quiet days and run out on busy ones. Industry estimates put prepared-food waste at 15 to 25 percent of production. We do not know our own figure. The first two weeks of this pilot would establish it.

What I am asking for

Sales historyat least two years

Daily sales by item for the pilot products, the ten highest-dollar prepared-food items, going back at least two years and three if the records exist. The tool learns from repeated seasons, so it needs to have seen every holiday and every month more than once. This is the movement report the store already produces, exported once and read-only. I would not need access to any live system.

A daily production countthe one I cannot do without

How many of each pilot item we put out each day. If a record already exists, a copy of it. If not, a photo of a clipboard each morning is enough. Waste is what we made minus what we sold, so without this number there is no baseline and no way to show a result.

A few facts per item

Retail price, cost, batch size (one tray, one bake) and shelf life. The department already knows these. I would also need store hours, any closures in the period, and the time of day the sales day closes in the system.

Paid hoursabout 80 at $30 an hour

Roughly $2,400 for the pilot, logged as used. This is skilled data work, and $30 is a fraction of what an outside contractor would charge for it. If payroll cannot run a project rate for an associate, the same total works as a one-time payment on top of my normal hours. Most of the time falls in the first six weeks, for connecting and checking the data and then running the daily sheet, and across the whole pilot it averages under an hour a day. Almost all of that work is the data connection, which costs the same whether we forecast five items or fifteen, which is why the pilot covers ten rather than five.

Two things in writingbefore any company data is used

Permission for read-only access to the items above, limited to this store and the pilot period. And a short signed note on ownership: I built the tool on my own time, it has never touched company data, and it stays mine. The store keeps the pilot results and can use them however it likes. A one-page letter is enough; I will draft it.

What follows if it passes

Success is defined below, and I would like it agreed now rather than after the fact. If the pilot meets it, I am asking for two things: a completion bonus at whatever level the store has discretion over, and for you to take the result to the district with your recommendation. Everything else on this page is within the store's authority. That last step is the one that is not, and it is where the real value of the pilot is decided.

What the store gets back

  • By the end of week two, this store's actual prepared-food waste in dollars per year. That is a measured figure rather than an estimate, and it is useful on its own whatever happens next.
  • By the end of week six, a report on how accurate the forecasts were against real sales, produced before anything in the kitchen has changed.
  • By the end of week thirteen, a measured before-and-after on waste and sellouts.

Kitchen operations do not change until week seven, and the manager can override any line on the sheet on any day.

What has already been done

  • A phone app for logging what gets thrown away. It is ready to use today.
  • A forecasting model, built and tested on a simulated store because I did not have company data and did not want to use any without permission. Over a full simulated year it cut waste by 37 percent with the shelves kept as full as they are today, and by 60 percent if we accepted a few more sellouts. Those are simulated figures. They show the approach works, not what this store will save, which is why I am committing to 10 to 15 percent for the pilot.
  • The step that reads a store's sales export. It is written and has been tested on a mock file seeded with the kinds of problems real exports have. It has not yet been run on real data.

How the pilot runs

Weeks 1–2

Measure. The department logs waste on the pilot items with the app. This produces the baseline.

Weeks 3–6

Watch. The tool prints a make-list every morning and the kitchen ignores it. Each day's forecast is scored against what actually sold.

Weeks 7–13

Follow. The kitchen uses the list. Waste and sellouts are measured against the baseline.

Before week seven, the forecasts have to pass five tests we agree on now, covering data quality, accuracy against the par sheet, and projected savings. If they fail any one of them, the pilot stops at week six and nothing on the floor has changed.

I propose that success means waste dollars down at least 10 percent from the baseline, with no meaningful drop in what is on the shelf.

What the kitchen sees

One printed sheet each morning. There is no new screen and no new system to learn.

MORNING SHEET Tue 2026-03-03 Store 0123 rain, high 44F ITEM MAKE BATCH NOTE Pizza slice 42 6 Rain: expect a slower lunch Rotisserie chicken 18 6 Hot bar (lb) 58 -- Sub sandwiches 24 8 Payday week CARRY-OVER ITEMS - NOT AN ORDER. CHECK WHAT IS LEFT FIRST. Bread loaf 30 10 3-day shelf life: one day's demand

What could go wrong

  • The forecasts may be less accurate on real data than they were in simulation. Weeks three to six exist to find that out before it costs anything.
  • The shelves could run short. The pilot uses the setting that keeps availability where it is today, and any item can go back to its par the same day.
  • The first real sales file will probably need some cleanup. The import step rejects bad data and says what is wrong with it. I have set aside the first week of the watch phase for this.
Two limits I want to be upfront about. Items that keep for several days, like bread and cake, get a reminder on the sheet rather than a make quantity. And items on a weekly ad will forecast less well than the rest, because the tool does not see the ad schedule.

The return

On its own the pilot is small: ten items, one store, ninety days. Its value is the number it produces, because that number multiplies. If the ten pilot items waste [$ baseline] a year at this store and the pilot cuts that by 10 percent, the store keeps [$ baseline × 0.10] a year on those items alone. The same ten items across [≈20] stores is twenty times that, and the ten items are only part of prepared foods. The tool works the same way for any item at any store, so the pilot is the cheapest possible way to find out whether that arithmetic holds. I would rather show the district a measured result from one store than an estimate for twenty.

I am happy to walk through any of this in person, and to adjust the pilot items or the timeline to suit the store.
