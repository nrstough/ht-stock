Executive summary

Prepared foods — bakery, pizza, hot foods, fresh cases — are produced against gut-feel par sheets. The result is a structural leak: industry-typical waste for these departments runs 15–25% of production, and the padding that "prevents" empty cases doesn't, because the pars can't see weather, holidays, or trend. I am asking for three things: written permission to access this store's sales data for a defined item list, a modest number of paid hours to run a 90-day single-store pilot, and an agreed recognition structure if the pilot hits its measured target — at least a 10% reduction in prepared-food waste with no loss of on-shelf availability. The forecasting system already exists: I built it, on my own time, using only synthetic data, and validated it in a full-year simulated trial. The pilot's downside is a few pallets of mis-produced food; the upside, extended across a district, is six figures a year.

The problem is two-sided, which is why it persists

Make too much and we eat the cost of goods. Make too little and we lose the margin and disappoint the customer standing at an empty hot bar at 6 pm. A par sheet solves this with a flat safety pad, but demand isn't flat: it swings with the day of the week, the season, the weather (a rainy Tuesday and a sunny Friday are different stores), and holidays — Super Bowl Sunday triples pizza demand; the day before a snow forecast, bread sells out no matter what we bake. A trailing average can't see any of that coming. A forecast can, because all of those drivers are known the day before.

What already exists

  1. A waste-measurement tool (Phase 1, deployed). A phone app for logging every discarded item with its retail value — it produces this store's real baseline number in two weeks of use. Baseline result: [$ — to be filled from the two-week log].
  2. A working forecasting system (proof of concept, complete). A demand model — a compact neural network of the same family used industrially for retail demand forecasting — plus a decision rule that turns each item's forecast into a morning production quantity. To keep every legal and data-policy question at zero, it was built and tested entirely on a synthetic store: three simulated years of sales with realistic weekday, seasonal, holiday, and weather structure, plus the noise no model should be able to predict.

Proof of concept: a year replayed

The model trained on the first two simulated years — seeing only what a real store sees, sales censored by sellouts — and then ran in shadow against the held-out third year. For every day and item, it recommended a quantity; the simulation's true demand, which no model ever sees, settled what would have sold, been wasted, and been missed. Two benchmark forecasters (a trailing average and a linear model) ran alongside so the network had to earn its seat, and an oracle with perfect knowledge of demand marked the best any method could do.

What the simulation proves — and what it doesn't

It proves the method: that demand with realistic weekday, weather, holiday, and seasonal structure is learnable from sales data alone, and that production driven by those forecasts beats a well-run par sheet by a wide margin. It does not prove this store's dollar figure — the simulated store is a plausible invention, and its simulated manager, though deliberately competent, is still an invention. That is why this proposal promises 10–15% in the real pilot, not 37–60%, and why Phase 1's measured baseline — not the simulation — is the number the pilot is judged against. No company data of any kind was used to build any of this.

The 90-day pilot

  1. Weeks 1–2 · Baseline. Log all discarded prepared-food items (5–10 pilot SKUs) with the Phase 1 tool. Output: this store's real waste number, in dollars per year.
  2. Weeks 3–6 · Shadow mode. With data access granted, the model retrains on this store's sales history and prints its morning sheet — but the kitchen ignores it. We record what it would have done. Output: a forecast-accuracy report against reality, before a single unit of production changes.
  3. Weeks 7–13 · Live. The kitchen follows the sheet (the manager can override it any day, and overrides are logged too). Output: measured waste and sellout change versus the baseline.

Success criterion, agreed in advance: at least a 10% reduction in prepared-food waste dollars versus the Phase 1 baseline, with no material drop in on-shelf availability. If shadow mode shows the forecasts aren't accurate enough, we stop at week 6 and nothing in the kitchen has changed.

The asks

  1. Written permission for read-only access to daily sales and production counts for the pilot items at this store, for the duration of the pilot.
  2. 40–80 paid hours of non-floor time to connect the store's data, run shadow mode, and operate the pilot.
  3. An agreed recognition structure, in writing, tied to the measured result — a bonus and/or a role that lets this scale beyond one store, settled before the live phase begins.

If it works

The simulated economics, discounted to the 10–15% this proposal actually promises and applied to a measured baseline of [$baseline]/year, pay back the pilot's hours in the first weeks of live operation. The system is one model serving every item — the same architecture scales from 9 SKUs at one store to every prepared-foods case in a district without redesign. At district scale ([≈20] stores), simulation-grade performance would be worth roughly $300K+ per year in avoided economic cost; even the conservative pitch number is six figures. The pilot is how we replace "roughly" with a measured fact.

Method notes. Demand model: a global quantile-regression neural network (GRU encoder over each item's trailing 28 days, learned item embeddings, calendar/weather/holiday covariates; pinball loss with censoring handling on sellout days, since sales only bound demand from below when the case sold out). Decision layer: newsvendor critical fractile per item, rounded to batch sizes. Validation: trained on simulated 2023–24, tested untouched on 2025 against naive, linear, and oracle policies. All simulation parameters, code, and data are in the project repository and reproducible with two commands.
