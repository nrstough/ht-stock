Executive proposal · Prepared foods · Harris Teeter

Fresh Forecast

Prepared-food waste is a forecasting problem, and forecasting has become cheap. This proposal asks for one pilot store, read-only data access and a modest number of hours to replace gut-feel par sheets with demand forecasts and to measure the result.

Executive summary

Bakery, pizza, hot foods and fresh cases are produced against par sheets that cannot see weather, holidays or trend. The padding those pars carry to avoid empty cases becomes waste; industry-typical waste in these departments runs 15–25% of production. A demand forecast can see every one of those drivers a day ahead.

A working forecasting system already exists. It was built on the author's own time on a fully synthetic store, so that no data-permission or IP question had to be answered before the method was proven. In a full simulated year it cut prepared-food waste by 37% with product availability held at today's level, and by 60% at the profit-optimal setting, closing 81% of the gap between today's practice and perfect knowledge of demand.

The decision requested is a 90-day pilot at one store with a stop gate at week 6, before anything in the kitchen changes. The pilot promises at least a 10% reduction in prepared-food waste dollars against a measured baseline with no material drop in availability. Its downside is a few pallets of mis-produced food; its upside, if it works, is a template for every store in the division.

The decision requested

  1. Approve one pilot store and name a sponsor at district or division level who owns the result.
  2. Grant written, read-only data access to daily sales and production counts for 5–10 pilot items at that store, for the duration of the pilot, revocable at any time.
  3. Fund 40–80 paid, non-floor hours to connect the store's export, run shadow mode and operate the live phase.
  4. Agree a recognition structure in writing, tied to the measured result and settled before the live phase, so that a successful pilot has somewhere to scale.

The problem is two-sided, which is why it persists

Make too much and we eat the cost of goods. Make too little and we lose the margin and disappoint the customer standing at an empty hot bar at 6 pm. A par sheet solves this with a flat safety pad, but demand is not flat. In the simulated store the demand index runs from 0.60 on a snowy Friday to 1.22 on a sunny one, hot foods swing nearly 40 points between July and January, and Super Bowl Sunday triples whole-pizza demand. A trailing average cannot see any of that coming. A forecast can, because all of those drivers are known the day before.

The reason this has persisted is that the two errors are charged to different ledgers. Waste shows up in shrink; a missed sale shows up nowhere. So the safe thing for a kitchen to do has always been to pad, and nobody has had a tool that priced the two errors against each other item by item.

What already exists

A waste-measurement tool (Phase 1, deployed)

A phone-based logger that records every discarded item with its retail value and produces a store's real baseline in two weeks of use. It is a digital clipboard for what a person observes being thrown away; it connects to nothing.

A working forecasting system (proof of concept, complete)

A demand model of the family used industrially for retail demand forecasting, forecasting a demand range for every item, plus a decision rule that turns each range into a morning production quantity by balancing the cost of one wasted unit against the cost of one missed sale. Cheap, high-margin items pad; expensive, low-margin items run lean, automatically. The output is a printed sheet in the kitchen each morning. No integration is required for a pilot: the inputs are a sales export, a public weather forecast and a calendar.

The real-data plumbing (built and rehearsed, unused)

An importer that turns a store's raw export into the model's input, a validation gate, training that takes about a minute per store, and the daily shadow-mode loop. All of it has been run end to end on a mock export built from the synthetic store with realistic dirt added. None of it has seen real data, and no permission has been sought yet: that ordering is deliberate.

Proof of concept: a year replayed

The model trained on the first two simulated years, seeing only what a real store sees, then ran in shadow against the untouched third year. For every day and item it recommended a quantity; the simulation's true demand, which no model ever sees, settled what would have sold, been wasted and been missed. Two benchmark forecasters ran alongside so the network had to earn its seat, and an oracle with perfect knowledge marked the best any method could do.

Economic cost is the total cost of being wrong: wasted units at cost of goods plus missed sales at lost margin. Forecast accuracy: 13.0% weighted error for the network against 15.5% for the trailing average and 15.1% for the linear model. The two benchmarks cut waste by running lean and paying for it in missed sales, which is why their economic cost barely moves.

The two forecast rows are the same model with a different availability target. Availability held is the pilot setting: $51,111 less food thrown away per year at retail value, with customers seeing an empty case about as often as they do today. Profit-optimal is where the decision rule would go on its own; the availability it gives up is a business choice to be made item by item, not a model setting. Savings accrue steadily through the year, about $300 a week per store in economic cost from the first week of live operation.

What the simulation proves, and what it does not

It proves the method: demand with realistic weekday, weather, holiday and seasonal structure is learnable from sales data alone, censored by sellouts as a real store's data is, and production driven by those forecasts beats a well-run par sheet by a wide margin. It does not prove this division's dollar figure. The simulated store is a plausible invention, and its simulated manager, though deliberately competent, is an invention too. That is why this proposal promises 10–15% in a real pilot rather than 37–60%, and why the pilot's measured baseline, not the simulation, is the number it is judged against. No company data of any kind was used to build any of this.

The 90-day pilot

Weeks 1–2

Baseline

Log every discarded prepared-food item across 5–10 pilot items with the Phase 1 tool.

Output: this store's real waste number, in dollars per year.

Weeks 3–6

Shadow mode

With data access granted, the model retrains on this store's history and prints its morning sheet. The kitchen ignores it; we record what it would have done.

Output: forecast accuracy against reality, scored against five go/no-go criteria fixed in advance.

Weeks 7–13

Live

The kitchen follows the sheet. The manager can override any line on any day; overrides are logged, not fought.

Output: measured waste and sellout change versus the baseline.

Success criterion, agreed in advance: at least a 10% reduction in prepared-food waste dollars versus the Phase 1 baseline, with no material drop in on-shelf availability. If shadow mode shows the forecasts are not accurate enough for this store, the pilot stops at week 6 and nothing in the kitchen has changed.

Governance and cost

  • Sponsor. One district or division leader owns the pilot, receives the weekly shadow-mode report, and makes the week-6 go/no-go call against the pre-agreed criteria.
  • Data. Read-only, scoped to the pilot items at one store, for the pilot's duration, revocable at any time. Nothing leaves company systems except the printed sheet. The request is made before any real data is touched.
  • Cost. 40–80 paid, non-floor hours, most of them in weeks 3–6. No software licences, no integration work, no hardware beyond a printer the kitchen already has.
  • Downside. Bounded to the live phase: a few pallets of mis-produced food over six weeks, at a store whose manager can revert any item to its par on any day.
  • IP and recognition. Nothing was built on company data, so ownership can be settled cleanly on the way in. The recognition structure is agreed in writing before the live phase.

If it works

One model serves every item, so the pilot is also the template. The same architecture scales from nine items at one store to every prepared-foods case in a district without redesign; retraining per store takes about a minute, and the morning sheet is the only interface a kitchen ever sees. Method notes. Demand model: a global quantile-regression neural network (GRU encoder over each item's trailing 28 days, learned item embeddings, calendar, weather and holiday covariates; pinball loss across 11 quantiles, with sellout days treated as censored since sales only bound demand from below when the case sold out). Decision layer: per-item newsvendor critical fractile, rounded to batch sizes; the availability-held variant raises each item's target until the year's fill rate matches the par sheet's. Validation: trained on simulated 2023–24, tested untouched on 2025 against naive, linear and oracle policies. The synthetic dataset, the trained checkpoint and the results file are frozen and checked in to the project repository, and the backtest reproduces the results byte for byte.
