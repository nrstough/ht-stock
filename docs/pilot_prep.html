Part 3. How it works, in the words you would use

This is the whole system, in the order the data moves through it. Read it twice. Then have me quiz you.

The one-sentence version

Every morning the tool looks at what each item sold over the last four weeks, what tomorrow is (weekday, season, holiday, payday, weather forecast), predicts a range for tomorrow's demand, and turns that range into a make quantity using the item's price and cost, so cheap high-margin items get padded and expensive low-margin items run lean.

The six pieces

1. IntakeReads the store's sales export, cleans it, and refuses anything it cannot explain.
2. FeaturesTurns each item-day into the numbers the model reads: recent history plus tomorrow's known facts.
3. Demand modelOne small neural network for all items. Outputs a range, not a number.
4. Decision rulePicks the quantity from the range using price and cost. Rounds to a batch.
5. Morning sheetPrints MAKE per item beside the store's own par, with a plain-language WHY.
6. ScoringRecords every prediction before the day happens, then grades it against real sales.

1. Intake

A store's sales export is messy in predictable ways: a report title above the header, a totals row below it, two-digit years, refunds printed in parentheses, a different barcode on every package of a weighed item, an item that changed number halfway through the history, days simply missing because nothing sold. The intake step reads that file through a mapping you write once (which column is the date, which is the item number, what date format, which item numbers are which products) and produces one clean table: one row per item per day, with units sold, units produced if known, and a sellout flag.

Its rule is that it never guesses. Every repair it makes has to be authorized by a named line in the mapping, and if something in the file is not covered, it stops and says which line would cover it. The reason is that a wrong guess produces a clean-looking table that trains the model on a typo, and nothing downstream would ever notice. A validation step then checks the table for gaps, items with too little history, closed days that look like demand collapses, and anything else that would quietly poison a forecast.

2. Features

For each item on each day the model is handed two things.

The first is the item's own last 28 days: how much sold each day (scaled so a doughnut and a cake are comparable), whether it sold out, whether the store was closed, the day's high temperature, and whether it rained or snowed. Six numbers per day, 28 days.

The second is what is known about tomorrow before it happens: which weekday, where in the year it falls, whether it is one of fourteen named holidays, how many days until the next holiday, the forecast high, the forecast weather kind, whether snow is forecast for the day after, whether it is a payday, and a slow trend term. About 35 numbers.

Every one of these is something a real store can see the evening before. Nothing from the future, and nothing from the simulator's hidden columns, is ever an input. That matters because it is the difference between a forecast and a cheat.

3. The demand model

One model serves every item. It has three parts. A small recurrent network reads the 28-day history and summarizes it. A learned "identity" for each item tells the model which product it is looking at, so the same network can know that doughnuts spike on weekends and hot bar spikes on cold days. And a small set of layers combines that summary, the item identity, and tomorrow's facts into the output.

The output is not one number. It is eleven numbers: the demand level the model thinks has a 5 percent chance of being exceeded, a 10 percent chance, 20, 30, 50, 70, 80, 90, 95, 97.5 and 99. Together they describe a range. The model is built so those eleven numbers can never cross, which is what makes them a range rather than a list.

It is trained to make each of those numbers honest: the 90 percent line should sit above actual demand about nine days in ten. There is one wrinkle that matters a great deal. On a day the case sold out, the sales figure is not demand, it is a floor on demand, because more would have sold. The training rule knows this: on sellout days the model is only penalized for predicting too low, never for predicting too high. Without that, a model learns that busy days are smaller than they are, and recommends too little on exactly the days that matter.

Sizes, in case you are asked: the model is small. It trains in about a minute on an ordinary laptop with three years of data for nine items. It needs no special hardware to run and produces a morning sheet in seconds.

4. The decision rule

A forecast is not a make quantity. The tool converts one into the other with a rule that is a century old and is the standard answer for perishables. For each item, the cost of making one unit too many is what that unit cost to make. The cost of making one too few is the margin you did not earn. The right quantity is the point in the demand range where those two risks balance. The formula is margin divided by price:

ItemPriceCostMargin ÷ priceWhat it means
Doughnut$1.19$0.250.79Make enough to cover demand on 79 days in 100. Cheap to over-make, so pad it.
Pizza slice$3.49$0.900.74Similar. High margin, pad generously.
Bread loaf$3.99$1.100.72
Rotisserie chicken$7.99$3.200.60Expensive to waste. Run closer to the middle of the range.
Sushi roll$8.99$3.800.58Leanest of the nine.

The tool reads that percentile off the eleven-number range and rounds up to the item's batch size, never below one batch. This is why a good forecast alone is not enough and why the item price and cost are on the list of things the store has to supply: a wrong cost gives a wrong quantity even with a perfect forecast.

5. The morning sheet

One printed page, 80 characters wide so a receipt printer can produce it. For each item: the MAKE quantity, the batch size, the store's own four-week same-weekday par beside it for comparison, and a WHY column that only ever states calendar and weather facts ("Fri; rain; payday"), never the model's reasoning, because a fact can be checked against a window and a wrong explanation destroys trust faster than a wrong number. Items that keep for several days are listed separately as a check-what's-left reminder rather than an order.

6. Scoring, and the five gates

Every prediction is written to a log before the sheet is printed, stamped with the time and how far the data ran. Nothing is ever re-forecast after the fact. The next day the real sales are joined to that log and the day is scored once. If the store later corrects a sales figure, the correction is recorded separately and the original score stands. This is what makes the four-week report a proof rather than a story.

Five pass/fail checks print on every weekly report from week one, so nobody can move the target later:

G1 Data complete
At least 95 percent of item-days scored, with no unexplained gap longer than a day.
G2 Beats the par
The model's error is at most 90 percent of the par sheet's error over the four weeks, and it beats the par in at least three of the four.
G3 Honest ranges
The middle line sits above actual demand about half the time, and the 90 percent line at least three quarters of the time.
G4 Worth doing
The provable waste saving is at least 15 percent of measured waste, with sellouts up by no more than three points.
G5 Covers the money
No more than two of the ten highest-dollar items were dropped for lack of history.

"Pending" is not "pass." A gate that cannot be measured, for instance because the store has no production count and so no waste figure, reads pending, and the pilot does not go live on a pending gate.

Accuracy, and what the benchmarks are

The accuracy number is weighted absolute percentage error, which you can explain as "on average, how far off in units, as a share of what actually sold." Lower is better. The model is always run alongside two simpler forecasters so it has to earn its place: a trailing average of the last four same weekdays (the par sheet's own brain), and a straightforward linear model that sees the same calendar and weather facts. On the simulated year the network scored 13.0 percent, the trailing average 15.5, the linear model 15.1.

The simulated store

You built and tested the model on an invented store, and you should say so plainly and early. The reason is legal and practical: it meant no company data was needed to settle whether the method works, and it means the tool arrives at the store with no history of touching anything it should not have.

What it is

Three years of daily records, January 2023 through December 2025, for nine prepared-food items at one mid-size store: whole pizza, pizza slice, doughnut, cake, bread loaf, rotisserie chicken, hot bar by the pound, sushi roll, and sub sandwich. One row per item per day, just under ten thousand rows. Every number in it is generated from a set of rules with a fixed random seed, so the same file can be regenerated byte for byte.

How demand is generated

Each item has a base daily rate, and a stack of multipliers turns that into a given day's expected demand:

  • A weekday shape per item. Doughnuts run 40 percent above their base on Saturday and 20 percent below on Tuesday. Hot bar is a weekday item. Whole pizza peaks Friday.
  • A yearly season per item. Hot bar is a winter item, sushi and subs peak in summer, bread and doughnuts peak around the holidays.
  • Fourteen named holidays, each with its own per-item effect. Super Bowl Sunday triples whole pizza and nearly doubles slices and subs. Mother's Day is two and a half times normal for cake. The day before Thanksgiving more than doubles bread. Thanksgiving itself runs at 45 percent for everything. Christmas is closed.
  • Weather. Rain takes 10 percent off traffic, snow takes 45 percent off, and the day before a forecast snow adds 25 percent to everything and 150 percent to bread. Hot bar and rotisserie sell more on cold days, sushi and subs on warm ones.
  • Paydays, the 1st to 3rd and 15th to 17th of the month, add 5 percent.
  • A slow traffic trend of 2 percent a year, and sushi growing 12 percent a year as a category.
  • A graduation-season window in May and June for cake.
  • Noise, on purpose. Every day has a store-wide random factor so items move together, each item has its own random factor, and about one day in a hundred is an unpredictable local event that adds 35 percent. No model can learn these, and the point is that no model should be able to.

How the simulated manager decides

This is the part to be ready to defend, because the first sharp question is "so you beat a dumb baseline." The status-quo manager is not dumb. Each morning it averages the last four same-weekday sales, adds 6 percent for every recent sellout because it knows sales undercount demand on sold-out days, and pads by 15 percent. If yesterday sold out it bumps 10 percent. If yesterday threw away more than 30 percent it trims 6 percent. It knows holidays are coming and adjusts in the right direction, just imprecisely. It bakes extra bread before snow and cuts back on snow days and rainy mornings. Over the test year it wasted 18.8 percent of production while keeping the shelves 98 percent stocked, which is a competent operator, not a straw man. What it lacks is precision: it lags trends and seasons by weeks, it guesses holidays coarsely, and it cannot see weather beyond a rule of thumb.

What the simulation can see that a real store cannot

The generator knows the true demand every day, including the sales that never happened because the case was empty. Those columns exist only to grade the policies afterward. The model never sees them during training and the real-data version of the code cannot read them at all. If asked "how do you know what would have sold," the honest answer is: in the simulation, because the simulation defines it. In a real store, nobody knows, which is why the real-data scoring reports measured waste exactly and reports lost sales only as a provable lower bound.

The replay

The model trained on 2023 and 2024 and was then run against 2025 as if live, one day at a time, recommending a quantity for every item on every open day. The simulation's true demand settled what sold, what was wasted, and what was missed. The same year was replayed under the status-quo manager, the two benchmark forecasters, the model at its profit-optimal setting, the model pinned to the manager's own availability level, and an oracle that knows the true demand distribution and marks the best any method could do.

The model closes 81 percent of the gap between the par sheet and perfect knowledge. Nothing closes all of it, because some demand is noise by construction.

What it proves and what it does not

It proves the method: demand with weekday, season, holiday and weather structure can be learned from sales alone, even with sellouts hiding the busy days, and production driven by that forecast beats a competent par sheet by a wide margin on both waste and total cost. It does not prove this store's number. The simulated store is a plausible invention with parameters someone guessed at, its manager is an invention, and its weather forecast is perfect where a real one is not. That is why the pilot promises 10 to 15 percent, why the store's own measured baseline is the yardstick, and why four shadow weeks come before anything changes in the kitchen.

The known limits, in your own words

  • Items that keep more than a day, like bread and cake, are served from a mix of today's and yesterday's stock. The tool's rule assumes each day starts from zero, so for those items it prints a reminder rather than a quantity. A proper fix is a separate piece of work.
  • Ads and markdowns are not modeled. An ad week looks like noise to the model and makes that item's range wider. If the store has a heavy ad cadence, that will be the main source of error.
  • If the store has no record of daily production, the tool cannot tell a sellout from a slow day, quantities run somewhat low on the busiest days, and there is no waste figure to measure savings against. It still runs and says so on every page.
  • The rehearsal on a mock export was written by the same person who wrote the intake step, so the first real file will break something nobody anticipated. That is a week of cleanup, not a failure, and it is budgeted.

Glossary, for your own reference

Par sheet
The fixed make quantity per item the kitchen works from today.
Item movement
The store report of units sold per item per day. The training data.
Sellout / stockout
The case ran empty. Sales that day are a floor on demand, not demand.
Censoring
The technical word for the sellout problem. The model is trained to treat sold-out days as "at least this much."
Quantile
One line in the demand range. The 90 percent quantile is the level demand stays under nine days in ten.
Newsvendor rule
The inventory rule that picks the quantity where the cost of one too many balances the cost of one too few. Margin divided by price gives the percentile.
Critical fractile
The newsvendor rule's percentile. Doughnut 0.79, rotisserie 0.60.
Batch
The unit production is rounded to. A tray of twelve doughnuts, a bake of four chickens.
WAPE
Weighted absolute percentage error. Total units off divided by total units sold. Lower is better.
Shadow mode
The tool prints its sheet, the kitchen ignores it, and the predictions are scored against real sales.
Baseline
The store's measured waste before anything changes. The yardstick.
Oracle
A policy that knows the true demand distribution. Exists only in the simulation, as a ceiling.
Fill rate / availability
Share of demand actually served. 98 percent means two units in a hundred were wanted and not there.
